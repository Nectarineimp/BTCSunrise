Market makers act like wholesale liquidity providers: whenever an investor wants to buy or sell an options contract, the market maker takes the other side of that trade. Because they profit from transaction fees rather than gambling on price swings, they immediately balance their risk by buying or selling the underlying asset. **Gamma** measures how fast their risk changes as the price moves, dictating how aggressively they must rebalance. When dealers hold positive gamma, they are forced to trade *against* market momentum—buying dips and selling rips to stay balanced—which smooths out price swings. When dealers hold negative gamma, they must trade *with* momentum—selling as prices fall and buying as prices rise—which supercharges volatility.

As weekly options expiries approach, the gamma profile around strike clusters ($80k vs. $85k) dictates whether market makers act as volatility dampeners or accelerators. Moving into positive gamma territory above $82,000 would cause dealer hedging flows to dampen intraday realized volatility, encouraging horizontal consolidation.
